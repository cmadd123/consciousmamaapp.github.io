Sippy Cup vs. Straw Cup

What to hand your toddler when the bottle goes away — and why it matters for teeth and speech.

Quick answer

Pediatric dentists and speech-language pathologists generally prefer straw cups (and open cups) over traditional hard-spout sippy cups. Straws encourage a more mature swallow and better oral-motor and tongue development, while heavy, prolonged use of spout sippy cups can affect tongue posture and — with sugary drinks that pool around the teeth — raise the risk of cavities. Spout sippy cups are still handy for spill control; many families use a straw or open cup for everyday drinking and keep a spout cup for the car or stroller.

Sippy cup (hard spout)

Spill-resistant and an easy step from the bottle. But the spout encourages a more immature suck, and prolonged use — especially with juice or milk — can affect oral development and teeth.

Straw cup

Promotes a mature swallow and good oral-motor development, and it's the option dentists and speech therapists usually recommend. Still spill-resistant, though it takes a little practice to learn.

Which should you choose?

  • You want the best option for teeth and speechStraw cup (or open cup)
  • You need the easiest bottle-to-cup stepSpout cup, short-term
  • Spills in the car or stroller are the concernA valved straw cup
  • Your child is ready for the next stepAn open cup with help at meals

Frequently asked questions

When should we switch from a bottle to a cup?

Common guidance is to introduce a cup around 6 months and aim to be off the bottle by about 12–18 months. Starting a straw or open cup early makes the transition smoother.

Are hard-spout sippy cups bad?

They're not harmful for occasional use, but relying on them heavily and for long periods — especially with sugary drinks — can affect oral development and teeth. Straw and open cups are better for daily drinking.

What about open cups?

Open cups (with help) are excellent for oral development and are recommended early — start with tiny amounts of water at meals. Expect spills while your child learns.

What's a good age for a straw cup?

Many babies can learn to use a straw cup around 9–12 months, and some earlier. It's fine to introduce it as soon as your child shows interest.

General guidance only — for concerns about oral development, speech, or your child's teeth, check with your pediatrician, pediatric dentist, or a speech-language pathologist.
