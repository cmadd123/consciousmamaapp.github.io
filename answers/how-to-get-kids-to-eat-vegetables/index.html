How do I get my kids to eat vegetables?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Offer vegetables often, in small amounts, next to foods your child already likes — it may take 10 or more tries[1]. Eat them yourself, let kids choose between two vegetables or help cook, add dips or a sprinkle of cheese, and skip nagging, bribing, and forcing[1][2]. Repeated tiny tastes measurably increase acceptance[3].

What works

  • Keep offering. It may take 10 or more tries before a child accepts a new food[1]. Serve small portions, paired with a food your child likes[1].
  • Model it. Eat family meals together and let your child see you enjoying a variety of vegetables[1].
  • Offer choices. Asking "green beans or broccoli?" gives children a sense of control[1].
  • Make it fun. Arrange vegetables into faces or shapes, or serve them with yogurt dip or a sprinkle of cheese[1][2].
  • Involve kids. Let them pick recipes and help with simple, supervised cooking tasks[2].
  • Use food bridges. Once a vegetable is accepted, try others with a similar color, flavor, or texture[2].
  • Pair new with familiar. Serve less-liked flavors, like bitter greens, next to familiar favorites[2].

What to avoid

  • Nagging, forcing, bargaining, or bribing — they create power struggles[1].
  • Using dessert as the reward for eating vegetables. It makes vegetables the chore and dessert the prize.
  • Giving up after a few refusals. Low-pressure repetition is what changes taste[3].
  • Serving raw, hard vegetables to young children — cook them well or cut them small to reduce choking risk[1].

Get kids involved

  • Let kids choose a vegetable at the store or farmers market.
  • Read kid-friendly cookbooks together and let your child pick a recipe to try[2].
  • Give them a supervised job: washing, tearing lettuce, or stirring[2].
  • Grow a pot of cherry tomatoes or herbs on a windowsill.
  • Let them build their own plate from a few vegetable options.

Plan vegetables into the week

Children learn to like vegetables through repetition, which is easier to deliver on purpose than by chance. In one trial, parents offered a tiny daily taste of a disliked vegetable for two weeks, and children ate and liked it significantly more than a control group[3].

  1. Pick two or three vegetables for the week and serve each several times.
  2. Put at least one vegetable at every dinner, even if it’s usually ignored.
  3. Rotate preparations of the same vegetable before switching to a new one.
  4. Look at progress over weeks. Eating a vegetable over a week, not at each meal, is what matters[4].

Blending vegetables into sauces can raise intake as well[5], but kids still need to see and taste vegetables to learn to like them.

Frequently asked questions

How many times should I offer a vegetable?

Often 10 or more[1]. Space tries a week or two apart at meals[2], or offer a tiny taste daily[3].

Is it okay to let kids eat vegetables with dip?

Yes. Dips like yogurt or hummus, or a sprinkle of cheese, are a common way to make vegetables more appealing[1].

My child eats fruit but no vegetables. Is that okay?

Fruit provides many of the same nutrients, so it’s a reasonable bridge. Keep offering vegetables alongside it without pressure, and mention it to your pediatrician if you’re concerned.

Should I hide vegetables in food?

Adding puréed vegetables to dishes can increase intake[5], but use it alongside visible vegetables, not instead of them.

Should I praise my child for eating vegetables?

Gentle praise for eating is fine — the NHS suggests praising children for eating, even if they only eat a little[4]. Avoid big rewards or bargains tied to vegetables.
