Picky eating

When is picky eating a problem?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Most picky eating is a normal phase: if your child is active, gaining weight, and seems well, they’re likely getting enough[1]. Talk to your pediatrician if your child isn’t growing or is losing weight, eats an extremely limited range of foods, shows fear or distress around eating, or if eating problems disrupt school, family, or social life[2].

Typical picky eating

It’s normal for toddlers to choose from a limited number of foods[3]. Food jags, refusing vegetables, and eating well one day and barely at all the next are all common. Looking at what your child eats across a week is more helpful than judging a single day[1], and a child who is growing well is getting enough to eat[4].

Typical picky eating vs. signs to raise with your pediatrician

Also talk to your pediatrician about ongoing gagging or vomiting, trouble chewing or swallowing, frequent constipation or tummy pain, or signs that your child seems unwell or unusually tired.

What is ARFID?

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is an eating disorder in which children are extremely selective eaters and sometimes have little interest in food[2]. Some react strongly to sensory features of food; some have a low appetite; some fear what might happen if they eat. Many children with ARFID are underweight[2]. It often starts younger than other eating disorders and is more common in children with anxiety, OCD, autism spectrum disorder, or ADHD[2].

ARFID is best treated by a team that can include a doctor, dietitian, and therapist, and most children can be treated at home[2]. Dealing with it early is the best way to treat it successfully[2]. Only a health professional can diagnose it.

How to prepare for the appointment

  • A one-week food log: every food and drink, roughly how much, and where.
  • A list of all the foods your child currently accepts.
  • Any foods your child used to eat and now refuses.
  • Notes on behavior at meals: gagging, distress, refusal, or fear.
  • Questions about growth, nutrition, and whether a dietitian or feeding specialist could help.

How to support a very selective eater at home

Whatever the cause, the same calm approach helps. Keep meals and snacks at regular times. Always include at least one accepted food. Let your child see, touch, and smell new foods without being asked to eat them. Avoid pressure, which can increase anxiety around eating. And work with your pediatrician and any specialists they recommend.

What to do now

  1. Keep a simple food log for a week: what your child eats, how much, and how meals go.
  2. Bring the log, and any concerns about growth or behavior, to your pediatrician.
  3. Meanwhile, keep meals calm and pressure-free. Serve one family meal with at least one food your child accepts[5].
  4. Avoid bribing or forcing[5], and don’t use food as a reward[1].
  5. Keep offering new foods in small amounts, alongside familiar ones[5].

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my child is eating enough?

If your child is active, gaining weight, and seems well, they are likely getting enough[1]. Your pediatrician tracks growth at well-child visits.

At what age do kids grow out of picky eating?

There’s no fixed age. For many children it eases through the preschool and early school years, especially with low-pressure, repeated exposure to foods.

Is picky eating linked to autism?

Picky eating is common in all children. ARFID is more common in children with autism spectrum disorder, anxiety, OCD, or ADHD[2]. If you have concerns about development, talk to your pediatrician.

Who treats severe picky eating?

Usually a team — a doctor, a dietitian, and a therapist who specializes in feeding or eating disorders[2]. Start with your pediatrician.

Can a picky eater get enough nutrients?

Often, yes — children who are growing well are generally getting enough[4]. If your child avoids whole food groups, ask your pediatrician whether a dietitian or a supplement would help.
