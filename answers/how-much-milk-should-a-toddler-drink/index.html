How much milk should a toddler drink?

Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

From 12 to 24 months, the AAP suggests about 2 cups (16 oz) of plain whole milk a day[1], and a 2019 expert consensus sets 2–3 cups as the range[2]. From age 2, switch to low-fat or skim, about 2 to 2½ cups a day[2]. More than that can crowd out food and make iron harder to absorb[3].

The sources differ slightly — the AAP’s parent page lists 16–24 oz for ages 2 to 5[1] — but they agree on the big picture: a couple of cups a day is plenty. Yogurt and cheese count toward dairy too[3].

One cup is 8 ounces. In practice, 16 ounces is a small cup of milk at breakfast, lunch, and dinner, or two slightly larger servings — not a bottle every few hours.

Why more milk isn’t better

Milk is nutritious, but it’s also filling. A toddler who drinks several bottles or cups a day may have little appetite left for meals. The CDC notes that too much milk can reduce hunger for other nutrient-rich foods and may make it harder to absorb iron[3]. If your toddler is a big milk drinker and a light eater, trimming milk is often the first fix.

Switching at 12 months can take some patience. Some babies take to cow’s milk right away; others need several tries. Offer it in a cup at meals, keep the amount modest, and let food do more of the work. If your child refuses milk entirely, yogurt and cheese can cover dairy needs[3].

What about plant-based milks?

  • Soy milk is the exception: the AAP considers it nutritionally equivalent to cow’s milk and an acceptable alternative[1].
  • Choose fortified, unflavored, unsweetened versions with added calcium and vitamin D, and check labels — nutrients vary by brand[3].
  • Most other plant milks (almond, oat, rice, coconut) aren’t recommended in place of dairy milk unless there’s a medical or dietary reason, because their nutrition isn’t equivalent[1][2].
  • Toddler milks or "transition formulas" are unnecessary for most children and often contain added sugars[1].

If your family is dairy-free, plan the rest of the diet with that in mind: protein from beans, eggs, tofu, meat, or fish, and calcium and vitamin D from fortified foods. A registered dietitian can check whether your toddler’s overall diet is covering what milk normally would.

What to do

  1. At 12 months, switch from formula to plain whole cow’s milk, offered in a cup with meals[1][3].
  2. Aim to finish moving off the bottle between 12 and 18 months[5].
  3. Offer water between meals — about 1–4 cups a day from 12 to 24 months[1].
  4. Skip juice before age 1 and keep it to no more than about 4 oz a day for toddlers; whole fruit is better[1].
  5. At age 2, switch to low-fat (1%) or skim milk unless your pediatrician advises otherwise[1][2].
  6. If your child has a dairy allergy, follows a vegan diet, or drinks much more or less milk than recommended, talk to your pediatrician about alternatives[3].

Frequently asked questions

Is 32 oz of milk too much for a toddler?

Yes — that’s well above recommendations of about 16–24 oz a day for 1- to 2-year-olds[1][2]. Large amounts can crowd out food and affect iron absorption[3].

Should my 1-year-old drink whole milk or 2%?

Whole milk is recommended for most children from 12 to 24 months[1][2]. Your pediatrician may suggest reduced-fat milk in some situations, such as a family history of heart disease or concerns about weight gain[3].

Can my toddler drink oat or almond milk instead?

Fortified soy milk is the only plant milk the AAP considers nutritionally equivalent[1]. Other plant milks are lower in protein and aren’t recommended as a main milk without a medical or dietary reason — talk with your pediatrician[2].

What if my toddler refuses milk?

That’s okay. Dairy can come from yogurt and cheese, and calcium and vitamin D from fortified foods; ask your pediatrician if you’re concerned[3].

When should my toddler stop drinking milk from a bottle?

Guidance is to finish moving off the bottle between 12 and 18 months, offering milk in a cup instead[5].
