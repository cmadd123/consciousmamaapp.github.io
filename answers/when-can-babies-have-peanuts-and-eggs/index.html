When can babies have peanuts and eggs?

Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

Most babies can start peanut and egg foods once they've begun solids, around 6 months — and there's no benefit to waiting, since delaying doesn't prevent allergies[1][3]. If your baby has severe eczema or has reacted to a food, especially egg, talk to your pediatrician first. Never give whole nuts; offer thinned peanut butter instead[1].

What current guidance says

Advice on allergenic foods has reversed over the past decade. Parents were once told to hold off on peanuts and eggs; now pediatric guidance encourages introducing them early. The AAP says that for babies without increased risk, peanut-containing foods ideally go in as early as 4 to 6 months, and that introducing allergenic foods later may raise the chance of an allergy[1]. The NHS similarly notes that delaying peanut and hen’s egg beyond 6 to 12 months may increase the risk of allergy[3].

The CDC puts it simply: introduce potentially allergenic foods when you introduce other foods[2]. That includes egg, peanut, dairy, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, and sesame.

The research behind the shift also stressed what happens after the first taste: babies who tolerate peanut should keep eating it regularly, in age-appropriate portions[1]. Introducing allergens is less a one-time event than a habit.

How to introduce peanut safely

  • Never give whole peanuts or tree nuts — they are a choking hazard until about age 4[1].
  • Thin a small amount of smooth peanut butter into infant cereal, fruit purée, or yogurt, or dissolve it in breast milk or formula and offer it by spoon[1].
  • Avoid thick spoonfuls of peanut butter, which can stick and cause choking.
  • Once tolerated, keep peanut foods in the diet regularly — research on early introduction stressed continued, routine servings[1][3].

How to introduce egg

  • Start with egg that is fully cooked — hard-boiled and mashed, or scrambled and cut into small pieces.
  • Offer a small amount at first, at home, at a time of day when you can watch your baby afterward.
  • Once tolerated, keep egg in the regular rotation[1][3].

What to do: a simple plan

  1. Start solids when your baby shows readiness signs, around 6 months[2][4].
  2. After a few first foods go well, introduce one allergenic food at a time, and wait at least a day before starting another new food[1].
  3. Offer it early in the day at home so you can watch for a reaction.
  4. Watch for hives or a red itchy rash, swelling of the lips or face, vomiting, or worsening eczema. Reactions usually appear within minutes but can take up to 2 hours[3].
  5. If you see mild symptoms, stop the food and call your pediatrician[1]. If your baby has trouble breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, call 911 — anaphylaxis is a medical emergency[3].

Frequently asked questions

Can I give my baby peanut butter at 4 months?

The AAP says peanut foods can ideally be introduced as early as 4 to 6 months for babies without increased risk, but only once your baby is developmentally ready for solids[1][4]. Most babies are ready around 6 months. If your baby has severe eczema or an egg allergy, check with your pediatrician first[1].

Do I need allergy testing before giving peanut?

Not usually. Testing is generally considered for babies at high risk — those with severe eczema or a prior food reaction — and your pediatrician will advise[1].

How often should my baby eat peanut and egg once they’re introduced?

Regularly. Guidance stresses keeping tolerated allergenic foods in your baby’s usual diet rather than offering them once and stopping[1][3].

What does an allergic reaction look like?

Common signs include hives or a red itchy rash, swollen lips or face, vomiting, and worsening eczema; wheezing or trouble breathing is an emergency[3].

What other foods are common allergens?

Besides peanut and egg, the NHS lists cow’s milk, wheat and other gluten grains, soya, sesame and other seeds, tree nuts, fish, and shellfish, among others[3]. Introduce them one at a time, like any new food.
