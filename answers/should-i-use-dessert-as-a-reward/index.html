Picky eating

Should I use dessert as a reward for eating dinner?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Generally no. The AAP and NHS both advise against using food as a reward[1][2]. Telling kids they can have a cookie if they eat their broccoli only reinforces the appeal of the cookie over the vegetables[3]. A better approach: decide when sweets are served and offer them without strings attached.

Why dessert rewards backfire

  • They make dinner a chore. If dessert is the prize, the meal becomes the price — and children learn that vegetables are something to get through[3].
  • They raise the status of sweets. Parents have used dessert as motivation for decades, but it teaches kids that treats are more valuable than mealtime food[4].
  • They create bargaining. "How many bites for dessert?" turns every meal into a negotiation.
  • They override hunger cues. Children who eat to earn a treat aren’t listening to whether they’re full.

Food should be nourishment, not a reward or punishment, and rewards or bribes usually cause more problems than they solve[1].

What the research and guidance agree on

Pediatric, public health, and dietitian guidance points the same way. The AAP says food should be used as nourishment, not as a reward or punishment[1]. The NHS advises against using food as a reward and suggests rewards like a trip to the park instead[2]. KidsHealth notes that dessert-for-veggies deals only reinforce the appeal of the cookie[3]. The shared idea is simple: children eat better when food isn’t tied to approval, bargaining, or prizes.

What to do instead

Instead of…Try…
"Eat your vegetables or no dessert."Serve dinner without mentioning dessert at all.
Dessert every night after a clean plateDessert on some nights, planned ahead, regardless of what was eaten.
Big dessert after dinnerA small portion served alongside the meal, occasionally.
Sweets as a reward for good behaviorNon-food rewards: a trip to the park or a game[2].
Bribes to try new foodsRepeated, low-pressure exposure.

When dessert stops being a prize, it loses some of its power. Many families find that children who know a treat is coming on Friday stop fixating on it every night.

Signs your current approach isn’t working

  • Every dinner starts with "Is there dessert?"
  • Your child negotiates bites: "How many do I have to eat?"
  • Meals end in tears when dessert is withheld.
  • Your child eats quickly and minimally just to get the treat.

If that sounds familiar, take dessert out of the deal for a couple of weeks. It usually takes a little while for children to stop bargaining once the rules change.

Planning treats into the week

Treats are part of normal family life. Deciding in advance when they happen takes the daily negotiation out of it.

  1. Choose which days will include a dessert or treat, and stick to it.
  2. Keep portions small and serve them the same way whether or not your child ate dinner.
  3. Include fruit-based desserts sometimes, like yogurt with berries or baked apples.
  4. Don’t remove planned treats as punishment.
  5. For children under 2, skip added sugars altogether — CDC advises no added sugars before 24 months[5].

More quick answers

  • What if grandparents use dessert as a reward? Explain your approach kindly and suggest alternatives, like a special activity together. Occasional differences at grandma’s house are unlikely to undo a calm routine at home.

Handling "Is there dessert?"

Answer simply and calmly: "Not tonight — we’re having strawberries on Saturday." If your child doesn’t eat dinner, avoid offering dessert, or a snack, until the next planned meal or snack time[1]. That way, skipping dinner doesn’t become a route to sweets.

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad to have dessert every night?

Not necessarily, but daily sweets add up. Many families do better with a few planned treat nights. The key is not making dessert conditional on eating dinner.

What if my child eats only dessert?

Serve a small portion so it can’t replace dinner, and keep offering the meal at the next scheduled time. Avoid giving extra food between planned meals and snacks[1].

Are non-food rewards okay?

Yes. The NHS suggests non-food rewards such as a trip to the park or a game[2].

Can I use food rewards for potty training?

Many experts advise against food rewards in general[1][2]. Stickers, praise, or a special activity are good alternatives.

Is it okay to give treats at parties and holidays?

Yes. Treats at celebrations are part of normal life. The goal is simply not to use them as payment for eating other foods.
