Why are family dinners important for kids?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Regular family meals are linked to healthier eating in children. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found kids who shared three or more family meals a week were more likely to be in a normal weight range, ate healthier, and had lower odds of disordered eating[1]. The meal doesn’t have to be dinner — breakfast together counts too[2].

What the research shows

In a 2011 meta-analysis covering more than 182,000 children and adolescents, researchers compared kids who shared at least three family meals a week with those who shared fewer[1]. Eating together three or more times a week was associated with:

  • 12% lower odds of overweight[1]
  • 20% lower odds of eating unhealthy foods[1]
  • 24% higher odds of eating healthy foods[1]
  • 35% lower odds of disordered eating[1]

These are associations from observational studies, so family meals are probably one part of a bigger picture — families who eat together may differ in other ways. Still, the pattern is consistent. Children whose families regularly eat together are also more likely to eat more fruits and vegetables[3].

Why shared meals help

  • Kids copy what they see. Eating with your child helps them learn by watching you[4].
  • It’s a natural place to introduce new foods alongside familiar ones[2].
  • It creates a routine, which helps children come to the table hungry rather than grazing.
  • It’s time to talk. The AAP suggests keeping conversation relaxed — no long lectures or pop quizzes[2].

How often — and how to make it realistic

Three family meals a week is a reasonable goal[1][2], and the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics suggests starting with even one[3]. You don’t have to eat every meal together, or even cook: weekend breakfast or brunch counts[2].

Conversation starters for the table

Talking is half the point of a family meal. A few low-pressure prompts help, especially with younger kids:

  • What was the best part of your day?
  • Who did you play with today?
  • If you could have any animal as a pet, what would it be?
  • What should we have for dinner next week?
  • What’s one thing that made you laugh today?

Keep it light. Mealtimes go better as a time for connection than for correcting table manners or reviewing homework.

More quick answers

  • Should kids help plan family meals? Yes. The AAP suggests picking a time each week to talk about what everyone would like to eat, and notes there’s a mealtime job for every age, from folding napkins to planning whole meals[2].

What to do this week

  1. Pick three meals you can realistically share — they don’t all have to be dinners.
  2. Plan what you’ll eat ahead of time, with at least one vegetable or fruit at each meal[2].
  3. Include one food each child usually eats, so nobody is stuck with nothing.
  4. Turn off screens and keep the mood light.
  5. Let kids help with an age-appropriate job.

Frequently asked questions

How many family dinners a week make a difference?

Research most often points to three or more family meals a week[1], and the AAP notes that even three meals together each week can make a real difference[2].

Does breakfast count as a family meal?

Yes. If weeknight dinners don’t work, weekend breakfast or brunch is a perfectly good family meal[2].

What if I don’t have time to cook?

You don’t have to cook from scratch to eat together[2]. Rotisserie chicken, a bagged salad, and bread is still a family dinner.

What age should kids start eating family meals?

As soon as they’re eating solids. Babies learn by copying, and sitting down together for family mealtimes is encouraged from the start[4].

Do family meals help picky eaters?

They can. Children learn by copying what they see adults eat[4], and shared meals are a natural place to put new foods next to familiar ones[2].
