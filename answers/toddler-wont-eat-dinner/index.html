Picky eating

My toddler won't eat dinner — what should I do?

Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

Usually, nothing drastic. Toddler appetites vary a lot, and skipping dinner now and then is rarely a problem for a child who is growing well[2]. Serve one family meal with at least one food they usually eat, skip pressure and bribes, and end the meal calmly. Then check whether snacks or milk are filling them up first[1][2].

Why toddlers refuse dinner

Dinner is the meal toddlers skip most, and the reasons are usually ordinary:

  • They aren’t hungry. An afternoon snack or a cup of milk too close to dinner can be enough to fill a small stomach. The AAP suggests spacing snacks at least 2 hours before meals[2].
  • They’re tired. By early evening many toddlers are running on empty. The NHS suggests not leaving meals until a child is too hungry or tired to eat[3].
  • Their appetite is naturally small. Growth slows after age 1, so toddlers need less food than many parents expect.
  • They’re asserting independence. Refusing food is one of the few things a toddler fully controls.
  • The food is unfamiliar. Many young children need 8 to 10 or more tries before they accept a new food[5].

The approach that works: a division of responsibility

A widely used feeding framework from dietitian Ellyn Satter splits the job in two: parents decide what food is served, when, and where; children decide whether to eat and how much[4]. It sounds simple, but it removes most dinner-table battles. Your job is to put a reasonable meal on the table at a predictable time. Your toddler’s job is to listen to their own hunger.

This lines up with AAP advice: let hunger guide eating, avoid pressuring or punishing, and don’t use treats as a reward for eating other foods — pressure tends to make children dislike foods they might otherwise accept[1].

In practice, that means dinner is served at roughly the same time each night, at the table, with a mix of new and familiar food. Whether your toddler eats three bites or thirty is up to them. Parents who switch to this approach often find refusals get shorter once there is nothing to push back against.

What to do tonight

  1. Serve one meal for the family, and include at least one food your toddler reliably eats (bread, rice, fruit, or a familiar side)[1].
  2. Put small portions on their plate and let them serve themselves more if they want it.
  3. Eat together without screens, and eat the food yourself — children learn by copying[1][3].
  4. If they refuse, don’t argue, coax, or cook a replacement meal. Calmly remove the plate without comment after a reasonable time[3].
  5. Don’t offer dessert or a snack as a trade for "three more bites." Keep the next snack at its usual time, not as a rescue meal[1].

What to change this week

  • Look at the afternoon. Move the last snack earlier, and offer water instead of milk or juice in the hour before dinner[2].
  • Try an earlier dinner, or make the afternoon snack more substantial and dinner lighter if your toddler is consistently too tired.
  • Keep re-offering refused foods in small amounts. Waiting a week or so between tries is fine[5].
  • Judge intake over the whole week, not one evening[3].

Frequently asked questions

Should I make my toddler something else if they won’t eat dinner?

The AAP advises against making a separate meal when your child refuses what was served — it tends to reinforce picky eating. Include one food they like in the family meal instead[1].

Is it okay to let my toddler go to bed without eating dinner?

For a healthy child who is growing well, an occasional skipped dinner is generally fine — they usually make it up at breakfast. If you prefer, a planned, plain bedtime snack can be part of the routine, as long as it isn’t offered as a reward for refusing dinner.

How long should I wait before ending the meal?

There’s no official number. Many families end the meal when the child is clearly done or the rest of the family finishes. Remove food calmly and without comment[3].

When is not eating dinner a sign of a problem?

Check in with your pediatrician if your toddler is losing weight or not growing as expected, seems ill, gags or vomits often with eating, or accepts only a handful of foods. Otherwise, picky eating is usually a normal stage[1].

Should I limit milk or juice before dinner?

If drinks seem to be filling your toddler up, yes. Offer water in the hour or two before dinner — the AAP recommends water instead of sugary drinks — and keep milk to recommended amounts[2].
