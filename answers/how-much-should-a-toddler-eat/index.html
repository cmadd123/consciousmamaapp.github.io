Toddler portions

How much should a toddler eat?

Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

Less than most parents expect. A toddler serving is roughly a quarter of an adult's — about 1 tablespoon of each food per year of age — spread across three meals and two or three snacks a day[1][2]. Appetite swings from meal to meal are normal; steady growth is the best sign they're getting enough[2].

How much food a toddler needs in a day

Growth slows after the first birthday, so toddlers need far less food than their energy levels suggest. The AAP estimates that children aged 1 to 3 need about 40 calories per inch of height each day — around 1,300 calories for a 32-inch toddler — though build and activity level shift that number up or down[1].

You don't need to count calories. A more practical rule: serve about one-quarter to one-third of an adult portion, or roughly 1 measuring tablespoon of each food for each year of age, and let your child ask for more[2].

Those servings are small on purpose. A typical toddler dinner might be an ounce of chicken (about two 1-inch cubes), a tablespoon or two of vegetables and fruit, and a quarter slice of bread[1]. If that looks like "barely anything," that is normal.

Why one day tells you very little

Toddlers often eat a big breakfast and pick at dinner, or eat well for three days and then live on air for one. Children usually eat when they're hungry and stop when they're full, so a small meal is not a problem on its own[2]. The NHS suggests looking at what your child eats over a week rather than a single day — if they're active, gaining weight, and seem well, they're likely getting enough[3].

Drinks count, too. Milk is filling: the AAP suggests about 2 cups (16 oz) of whole milk a day from 12 to 24 months, plus water, and no juice before age 1[4]. A toddler who drinks far more milk than that often has less room for food.

What to do

  1. Serve three meals and two or three planned snacks, spaced so snacks end at least 2 hours before the next meal[2].
  2. Start with small portions — about 1 tablespoon per year of age of each food — and offer seconds if they ask[2].
  3. Include at least one food your child usually eats at every meal, alongside what the family is having[3].
  4. Offer water between meals and keep milk to recommended amounts so it doesn’t replace food[4].
  5. Let your child decide how much to eat from what you serve. Skip pressure, bribes, and "clean plate" rules[3].
  6. Check growth at well-child visits rather than judging by individual meals[2].

Frequently asked questions

How many calories does a toddler need?

The AAP estimates about 40 calories per inch of height per day for children aged 1 to 3 — roughly 1,300 calories for a 32-inch toddler — but needs vary with build and activity[1]. Most parents never need to count; watch growth instead.

Is it normal for my toddler to eat a lot one day and almost nothing the next?

Yes. Day-to-day swings are common. Look at intake over a week; a child who is active, gaining weight, and well is usually getting enough[3].

Should I make my toddler finish their plate?

No. Children generally eat until they are full, and pressure can backfire[2][3]. Serve small portions and let them ask for more.

When should I worry about how little my toddler eats?

Talk to your pediatrician if your child is losing weight, falling off their growth curve, seems unwell or unusually tired, or eats a very narrow range of foods for a long stretch. Otherwise, small appetites are usually normal.

Should toddlers eat the same food as the rest of the family?

In most cases, yes. Serve family meals in toddler portions, leave out added salt, and cut food to a safe size. Eating the same food together also helps children learn to accept it[3].
