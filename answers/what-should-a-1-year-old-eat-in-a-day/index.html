What should a 1-year-old eat in a day?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

About 1,000 calories spread over three meals and two snacks[1], drawn from grains, fruits, vegetables, protein foods, and dairy. A typical day might include an egg or fortified cereal, half a sandwich, soft cooked vegetables, fruit, and 2 to 3 ounces of meat at dinner[2], with about 16 ounces of whole milk plus water to drink[3].

What a 1-year-old needs

Around the first birthday, most babies are eating three meals and two snacks a day, totaling about 1,000 calories[1]. Fat matters at this age: babies and young toddlers should get about half of their calories from fat, so the AAP advises against restricting fat from a 1-year-old’s menu[1]. Healthy fats come from foods like avocado, olive oil, fish, nut butters, and dairy[2].

Appetite varies a lot from day to day. That’s normal — look at what your child eats across a week rather than at a single meal.

A sample day for a 1-year-old

This menu is adapted from the AAP’s sample for a 1-year-old weighing about 21 pounds[2]. Treat amounts as a rough guide, not a target.

Sample menu (about 21 lb child)
MealWhat to offer
Breakfast½ cup iron-fortified cereal or 1 cooked egg; ½ banana, sliced; 2–3 sliced strawberries; ½ cup whole milk[2]
Morning snack½ cup yogurt with cut-up fruit, or toast with a thin spread of cream cheese or nut butter; water[2]
Lunch½ sandwich with turkey, chicken, tuna, or egg; ½ cup cooked green vegetables; ½ cup whole milk[2]
Afternoon snack1–2 oz cubed cheese, or 2–3 tbsp fruit; water[2]
Dinner2–3 oz cooked meat, ground or diced; ½ cup cooked orange or yellow vegetables; ½ cup whole-grain pasta or potato; ½ cup whole milk[2]

Many 1-year-olds eat less than this on a given day, and some eat more. If your child is growing well, small swings are fine.

Drinks at 12 months

DrinkHow much
Whole milkAbout 16 oz (2 cups) a day from 12 to 24 months[3]
Water8–32 oz (1–4 cups) a day[3]
JuiceNot needed; whole fruit is preferred[3]. CDC advises no more than 4 oz of 100% juice a day for ages 1 to 3[5]
AvoidFlavored milks, toddler milks, sugary drinks, and caffeine[3]

Easy swaps when your child refuses

  • Won’t eat meat? Try eggs, beans, lentils, or flaked fish for protein and iron.
  • Won’t eat vegetables? Keep offering them, and serve fruit alongside — many of the same nutrients are in both.
  • Won’t drink milk? Yogurt and cheese also provide dairy.
  • Won’t eat lumpy textures? Offer soft finger foods they can control, cut small.

Keeping meals safe

  • Mash food or cut it into small, easily chewable pieces[1] — no larger than half an inch[4].
  • Quarter hot dogs lengthwise, then slice; quarter cherry tomatoes; never offer whole grapes or peanuts[1].
  • Spread nut butter thinly on toast or crackers rather than serving it by the spoonful[1].
  • Make sure your child eats only while seated and supervised by an adult[1].
  • Skip added sugars until at least age 2[5].

What to do

  1. Serve three meals and two snacks at roughly the same times each day.
  2. Offer the family’s food in small portions, cut to a safe size.
  3. Include an iron-rich food at most meals: meat, eggs, beans, or fortified cereal.
  4. Switch from formula to whole milk at 12 months, offered in a cup with meals.
  5. Let your child decide how much to eat. Encourage, but don’t pressure or force[1].

Frequently asked questions

How much should a 1-year-old eat at each meal?

Portions are small: the AAP sample menu uses amounts like ½ cup of cereal, ½ banana, and 2 to 3 ounces of meat at dinner[2]. Let your child ask for more rather than aiming to finish a set amount.

Should a 1-year-old drink whole milk or 2%?

Whole milk is recommended for most children from 12 to 24 months[3]. Your pediatrician may suggest lower-fat milk for some children.

Can my 1-year-old eat what we eat?

Mostly, yes — in small, soft, safely cut pieces, without added salt or sugar[1][5].

My 1-year-old barely eats. Should I worry?

Appetite naturally slows after the first year. If your child is growing well and active, it’s usually fine. Talk to your pediatrician if you notice weight loss, very few accepted foods, or signs of illness.
