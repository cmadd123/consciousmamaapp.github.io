Picky eating

Should I make separate meals for my picky eater?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

No. Pediatric guidance recommends serving one meal for the whole family and resisting the urge to cook something else when your child refuses[1][2]. Instead, include at least one food your child usually eats in every meal[3], keep portions small, and let your child decide how much to eat from what’s on the table[4].

Why short-order cooking backfires

Making a separate dinner feels kind in the moment, but it teaches children that refusing the family meal produces something they like better. The AAP is blunt: serve one meal for the whole family and resist making another if your child refuses[1]. Cooking a different meal for each child turns you into a short-order cook[2].

It also removes the exposures that help children accept new foods. A child who always gets chicken nuggets never gets the chance to see, smell, and eventually taste what everyone else is eating.

How to make one meal work for everyone

  • Include a "safe" food at every meal. Something your child reliably eats — bread, rice, fruit, or cheese — means they won’t go hungry[2][3].
  • Serve meals family style or deconstructed, so children can choose what goes on their plate.
  • Keep sauces, dressings, and spicy toppings on the side.
  • Put small amounts of everything on your child’s plate, without pressure to eat it.
  • Let your child decide whether and how much to eat from what you serve[4].
  • If your child doesn’t eat, take the food away without comment and offer it again another day[5].

When different food makes sense

"One meal" doesn’t mean "one texture for everyone." Babies and young toddlers need food that’s mashed or cut to a safe size. Children with food allergies or medical diets need meals that are safe for them. And if your child eats an extremely narrow range of foods, is losing weight, or shows distress at meals, talk to your pediatrician — that may be more than typical picky eating.

What to say at the table

  • "This is what’s for dinner tonight. You can eat what you like from the table."
  • "You don’t have to eat it. You can leave it on the side of your plate."
  • "The kitchen is closed after dinner. Snack is at the usual time."
  • "We’ll have your favorite on Thursday."

Calm, neutral language keeps mealtimes from becoming a battle[1].

What to do

  1. Plan the week’s dinners with your picky eater’s safe foods in mind.
  2. Stop offering on-demand alternatives. Tell your child kindly what’s for dinner and that this is the meal.
  3. Invite your child to help shop for, choose, and prepare food[3].
  4. Avoid bribes and rewards for eating[1].
  5. Eat together and model eating the foods yourself.
  6. Give it a few weeks. Children often test the new rule before they settle into it.

Frequently asked questions

What if my child goes to bed hungry?

A healthy child who skips one dinner will usually eat well at the next meal or snack. Including a safe food at every meal makes it unlikely your child goes without anything[2][3].

Can I offer a backup like cereal or a sandwich?

Routine backups tend to become the real dinner. Make the backup part of the meal instead: bread, fruit, or another familiar food on the table[2].

Should I make my child take a bite?

Pressure often backfires. Some experts suggest asking for a couple of bites, but it’s fine if your child chooses not to eat more[3]. Repeated, low-pressure exposure works better than forcing.

How do I handle siblings with different tastes?

Rotate whose favorite anchors each night, and use build-your-own meals like tacos or bowls where everyone assembles their own plate.

What about kids who need different textures?

Adapt the family meal instead of making a new one. Mash, chop, or blend your baby’s or toddler’s portion so the whole table shares the same food in different forms.
