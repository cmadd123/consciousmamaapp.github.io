When can babies have salt, and how much is too much?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Babies don’t need added salt. NHS guidance sets a limit of less than 1 g of salt a day under age 1 and no more than 2 g a day from 1 to 3 years, because young kidneys can’t process much[1]. Don’t add salt, stock cubes, or gravy to baby food[2], and check labels on packaged foods[3].

Salt limits by age

NHS maximum daily salt intake
AgeMaximum salt per day
Under 1 yearLess than 1 g[1]
1–3 yearsNo more than 2 g[1]
4–6 yearsNo more than 3 g[1]
7–10 yearsNo more than 5 g[1]
11 years and olderNo more than 6 g (about 1 teaspoon)[1]

Babies shouldn’t have much salt because their kidneys aren’t fully developed and can’t process it[1].

Where salt hides in family food

The biggest sources usually aren’t the salt shaker. CDC lists these as high-sodium foods to limit for young children[3]:

  • Some canned foods — choose low-sodium or no-salt-added versions[3]
  • Processed meats like lunch meats, sausages, hot dogs, and ham[3]
  • Some frozen dinners[3]
  • Some snack foods and packaged foods made for toddlers[3]

Stock cubes, gravy, and many sauces are salty too, which is why the NHS advises keeping them out of baby food and cooking water[2].

Reading labels for sodium

Packaged foods list sodium on the Nutrition Facts label. CDC suggests using that label to find foods with less sodium[3]. Practical rules of thumb:

  • Compare two similar products and choose the one with less sodium per serving.
  • Look for "low sodium" or "no salt added" on canned vegetables, beans, and tomatoes[3].
  • Check serving sizes — a toddler snack bag may contain more than one serving.
  • Be cautious with processed meats, which are often high in sodium[3].

Salt for toddlers

After the first birthday, the limit rises only a little, to no more than 2 g a day for ages 1 to 3[1]. Toddlers can share family meals, so cooking with less salt for everyone is the easiest way to stay within it[4].

Low-salt family meals that work for babies

  • Homemade tomato sauce with garlic and herbs, over pasta.
  • Roast chicken with lemon and herbs, potatoes, and carrots.
  • Lentil soup made with water and vegetables instead of stock cubes.
  • Baked salmon with sweet potato and green beans.
  • Bean and vegetable chili, spices added after serving the baby’s portion.

Older family members can add salt at the table, so one pot works for everyone.

More quick answers

  • What happens if my baby has too much salt? An occasional salty food isn’t a cause for alarm. The concern is a regular pattern, since young kidneys can’t process much salt[1]. If your baby has eaten something very salty and seems unwell, call your pediatrician.

How to cook for the family without the salt

  1. Cook the family meal without salt, then add it at the table for older kids and adults.
  2. Take out your baby’s portion before adding stock, soy sauce, or seasoning mixes.
  3. Use herbs, garlic, onion, lemon, and mild spices for flavor instead.
  4. Choose no-salt-added canned beans and tomatoes, or rinse canned beans.
  5. Compare Nutrition Facts labels and pick the lower-sodium option[3].
  6. Keep salty foods like bacon, ham, and salty snacks as occasional extras for toddlers, not everyday foods.

Toddlers can eat much of what the family eats, and there’s no need to add salt to their food[4].

Frequently asked questions

Can I add a pinch of salt to my baby’s food?

It’s best not to. The NHS advises not adding salt to baby food or cooking water[2].

Is bread or cheese too salty for babies?

Everyday foods like bread and cheese contain some salt. They’re fine in sensible amounts as part of a varied diet — the limit is about the day’s total[1]. Go easy on very salty foods like processed meats[3].

When can my child eat the same salted food as us?

Even for toddlers, there’s no need to add salt[4]. Keeping family cooking lower in salt helps everyone, and NHS limits for children remain lower than for adults through age 10[1].

Are store-bought baby and toddler snacks low in salt?

Not always. CDC notes that some packaged foods for toddlers are high in sodium, so check the label[3].

Can babies have stock or gravy?

The NHS advises against adding stock cubes or gravy to baby food or cooking water, because they’re high in salt[2]. Low-salt homemade versions, used sparingly, are a better choice for the family pot.
