Picky eating

My toddler only wants snacks — what should I do?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Put snacks on a schedule. Offer three meals and two or three planned snacks at set times, with snacks at least 2 hours before the next meal[1], and offer only water in between[2]. Toddlers who eat all day may not learn what being hungry or full feels like[3], so they arrive at meals already full.

Why toddlers fill up on snacks

Snacks are easy to eat, familiar, and often handed out on demand — in the car, at the park, whenever someone asks. A toddler who grazes all afternoon simply isn’t hungry at dinner. Milk and juice count too: big cups of either right before a meal can blunt appetite[3].

The fix isn’t cutting snacks out. Toddlers have small stomachs and often can’t make it from lunch to dinner without one[4]. The fix is structure: you decide when food is offered, and your toddler decides how much to eat[2].

Snacks are spaced at least 2 hours before meals[1]. Set times for meals and snacks, and try to stick to them[3].

Make snacks mini meals, not treats

When snacks are planned, they can do real nutritional work. The AAP suggests combining a protein food (including dairy), a fruit or vegetable, and a grain or starchy vegetable — though not every snack needs to be a mini meal[4].

  • Plain yogurt with diced fruit and a piece of toast[4]
  • Oatmeal with peanut butter and shredded apple[4]
  • Cheese cubes, crackers, and cucumber
  • Hummus with soft pita and pepper strips
  • Hard-boiled egg and berries

Serve snacks at the table or in a set spot, not on the go, so they feel like a meal and have a clear end.

Common snack traps

  • Snacks on demand: offering food whenever it’s asked for teaches toddlers to ask often.
  • Snack cups on the go: constant nibbling in the car or stroller replaces meals.
  • Big drinks before meals: milk or juice right before dinner can blunt appetite[3].
  • Packaged snacks as the main snack: they’re convenient but filling and often less nutritious than a mini meal.
  • Snack as a backup for a skipped meal: it teaches toddlers to skip meals.

What a good day looks like

Success doesn’t mean your toddler eats everything at every meal. It means they come to the table hungry, eat something at most meals, and stop asking for food constantly between them. A toddler who eats a small lunch and a decent dinner after a planned snack is doing fine. Judge the week, not the day, and look for steady growth at checkups.

What to do

  1. Write down your toddler’s current eating times for two days. Most parents are surprised.
  2. Set a schedule of three meals and two or three snacks[1].
  3. Offer only water between those times[2].
  4. Keep packaged snacks out of sight and serve snacks on a plate.
  5. At meals, include one food your toddler likes, and serve small portions.
  6. Don’t make up for a skipped meal with an extra snack. Wait for the next scheduled time.
  7. Expect a few rough days. Most toddlers adjust within a week or two once they learn the rhythm.

Frequently asked questions

How many snacks should a toddler have?

Most toddlers do well with two or three planned snacks alongside three meals[1]. Many need an afternoon snack; some also need a morning one[4].

What if my toddler refuses dinner and asks for a snack later?

Calmly say the kitchen opens at the next snack time, then offer a planned snack then. Avoid replacing the meal with a snack right away.

Is it okay to give snacks in the car or stroller?

Occasionally. As a habit, on-the-go snacking makes it hard to tell when your toddler is hungry or full[3]. Seated snacks are also safer for choking.

Does milk count as a snack?

It can fill a toddler up like one. Avoid giving cups of milk or juice right before meals[3], and keep milk to recommended amounts.

What if my toddler cries for snacks between times?

Stay calm and consistent. Offer water and a reminder of when the next snack is. Most toddlers adjust when the routine is predictable[3].
