Starting solids

Can my baby eat the same food as the rest of the family?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Yes, once your baby is eating solids, most family meals can be adapted. Cook without added salt, sugar, stock cubes, or gravy[1], then mash or cut your baby’s portion to a safe texture and size[2]. Skip honey before 12 months[3]. By about 10 months, many babies eat three meals a day alongside their milk[1].

Why eating together helps babies

Babies learn by copying. The NHS encourages sitting down together for family mealtimes as much as possible[1], and by 10 to 12 months, lunches and dinners can include a main course and a dessert of fruit or unsweetened dairy, moving closer to the way older children eat[1]. Sharing your meal also means one dinner, not two.

Start smooth and build up: as your baby becomes a more confident eater, offer mashed, lumpy, and finger foods[1]. Food for young children should be cut into pieces no larger than half an inch[2].

Family dinners that adapt easily

Some meals are naturally baby-friendly with very little extra work:

  • Soups and stews: blend or mash a portion before seasoning.
  • Pasta with tomato or vegetable sauce: blend or finely chop for younger babies.
  • Shepherd’s pie or cottage pie: soft mince and mashed potato.
  • Fish with potatoes and vegetables: flake, check for bones, and mash.
  • Lentil dal: naturally soft; leave out the salt.
  • Omelets and frittatas: well-cooked egg, cut into fingers.

Meals that are harder to adapt include very salty dishes, fried foods, and anything with whole nuts, which aren’t safe as a choking hazard.

Texture progression at a glance

Smooth purées come first, then mashed food with soft lumps, then soft finger foods, and by the end of the first year a wide range of tastes and textures[1]. Move at your baby’s pace — some babies take to lumps quickly and others need more time.

What to leave out of your baby’s portion

  • Salt. Babies under 1 should have less than 1 g of salt a day, because their kidneys can’t process much[4]. Don’t add salt, stock cubes, or gravy to their food or cooking water[1].
  • Honey, until after 12 months — it can cause infant botulism[3].
  • Added sugars. CDC advises no added sugars for children younger than 24 months[3].
  • High-sodium foods such as processed meats, some canned foods, and some frozen dinners[3].
  • Choking hazards: whole grapes, popcorn, nuts, raw vegetable sticks, and hard chunks[2][5].

What to do

  1. Plan family meals you can cook without salt, then season the adult portions at the table.
  2. Take out your baby’s portion before adding salt, spice, or sauces.
  3. Mash, blend, or cut it to a texture and size your baby can manage.
  4. Offer a new food every few days so you can watch for reactions[5].
  5. Sit down together and let your baby watch you eat the same foods[1].
  6. Keep breast milk or formula going — solids add to milk in the first year, they don’t replace it.

Frequently asked questions

At what age can babies eat family food?

Babies can start adapted family foods as soon as they begin solids, around 6 months, with texture adjusted to their stage. By about 10 months, many are eating three meals a day[1].

How much salt is too much for a baby?

The NHS sets a limit of less than 1 g of salt a day for babies under 1[4]. The simplest way to stay under it is not to add any salt to their food[1].

Can babies have spices and herbs?

Mild herbs and spices are generally fine and can make food more interesting. The things to avoid are added salt, sugar, and honey[1][3].

Can my baby eat takeout or restaurant food?

Occasionally, but restaurant food is often salty. A small portion of plain components — rice, plain vegetables, unseasoned meat — is the better choice.

Do I need to buy special baby food?

Not necessarily. Many babies eat well on mashed or chopped family food made without salt or sugar[1]. Store-bought baby food is a convenient option, not a requirement.
