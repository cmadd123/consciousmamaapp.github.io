Is it okay to hide vegetables in kids’ food?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Yes, as an add-on, not a replacement. In a study of preschoolers, puréeing extra vegetables into meals raised daily vegetable intake by 50–73% without reducing how much they ate of vegetable side dishes[1]. But children learn to like vegetables by repeatedly seeing and tasting them[2], so keep serving visible vegetables too.

What the research shows

In a 2011 Penn State study, 40 children aged 3 to 5 were served all their meals and snacks one day a week for three weeks[1]. Researchers increased the amount of puréed vegetables in entrées like pasta and casseroles, tripling or quadrupling the vegetable content.

  • Daily vegetable intake rose by 50% with triple the hidden vegetables and 73% with quadruple[1].
  • Children ate just as many vegetable side dishes as before — hidden vegetables didn’t replace visible ones[1].
  • Children ate a similar weight of food, so daily calories dropped by about 12%[1].
  • Children rated the modified foods about the same[1].

It was a small, short, controlled study, so treat it as encouraging rather than conclusive.

Why hiding alone isn’t enough

Hidden vegetables add nutrition, but they don’t teach children to accept vegetables they can see. Acceptance comes from repeated exposure. In a randomized trial, parents who offered a tiny daily taste of a disliked vegetable for 14 days saw large increases in their child’s intake and liking of it[2]. The AAP notes it can take 10 or more tastes before a toddler accepts a food[3].

Think of it as two jobs. Blended vegetables in sauces quietly raise intake. Visible vegetables on the plate build familiarity. You want both.

Tips for adding vegetables well

  • Start with mild, soft vegetables that blend smoothly.
  • Add a little at first, then more as your family gets used to the taste.
  • Match colors: orange vegetables in orange sauces, green in green.
  • Cook vegetables until very soft before blending for the smoothest texture.
  • Make extra sauce and freeze it in portions for busy nights.

Hiding vs. honesty

There’s no need to announce every vegetable, but avoid outright lying if your child asks. If a child discovers a "trick," they may become suspicious of foods they used to eat. "There’s squash in the sauce — that’s why it’s so orange" is honest and builds familiarity. Some families call it "boosting" rather than hiding.

What to do

  1. Add puréed or grated vegetables to a few regular dishes each week.
  2. Still serve a small portion of a visible vegetable at every dinner.
  3. Offer tiny, low-pressure tastes of vegetables your child doesn’t yet eat[2].
  4. Eat vegetables yourself and keep mealtimes calm.
  5. Avoid nagging, forcing, or bribing[4].

Frequently asked questions

Do hidden vegetables count as a serving?

They do add vegetables to the diet. In the preschool study, the extra amounts were substantial[1]. Visible vegetables are still important for building acceptance.

Will hiding vegetables make my child pickier?

There’s little research on this. Being honest when asked, and continuing to serve visible vegetables, avoids most problems.

What vegetables are easiest to hide?

Mild, soft vegetables — cauliflower, zucchini, carrot, butternut squash, and spinach — blend easily into sauces, soups, and baked goods.

Should I hide vegetables for a baby?

Babies are learning tastes, so it helps to offer single vegetables on their own first. Mixed dishes can come later.

Do hidden vegetables reduce calories?

In the preschool study, children ate a similar weight of food, so adding vegetables lowered daily calories by about 12%[1]. That’s a side effect, not a reason to restrict children’s food.
