What are the best iron-rich foods for babies?

Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

Good first iron foods include iron-fortified infant cereal, soft-cooked meat and poultry, fish, eggs, beans and lentils, and tofu[1]. Health guidance recommends iron-containing foods as soon as solids start, around 6 months; babies 7 to 12 months need about 11 mg of iron a day[1][2]. Pairing plant sources with vitamin C foods helps absorption[1].

Why iron matters when solids start

Iron is a mineral the body needs for growth and development; it is used to make hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen[2]. The CDC recommends choosing iron-containing foods when your baby starts solids at about 6 months, and notes that breastfed babies may need an iron supplement even before then — ask your pediatrician[1].

AgeRecommended iron per day
Birth–6 months0.27 mg (mostly from breast milk or formula)[2]
7–12 months11 mg[2]
1–3 years7 mg[2]
4–8 years10 mg[2]

The 7–12 month figure is higher than the toddler one — this is the stage when iron from food matters most.

Heme iron, found mainly in meat, poultry, and fish, is absorbed more easily than non-heme iron from plant and fortified foods[1][2]. Non-heme sources still count — they just work better with a little help.

You don’t need to serve all of these. Two or three iron foods your family already eats — for example, fortified cereal at breakfast, beans or lentils at lunch, and a little meat or fish at dinner — rotated through the week, cover most babies well.

How to boost iron absorption

  • Pair plant iron with vitamin C: offer fortified cereal with mashed strawberries, lentils with tomato, or beans with sweet potato or broccoli[1][2].
  • Combine plant and animal sources: a little meat or fish alongside beans or greens helps the body absorb the plant iron[2].
  • Watch milk amounts after age 1: too much cow’s milk can make it harder to absorb iron and crowd out iron-rich foods[4].

Signs worth raising with your pediatrician

Low iron can develop slowly and quietly. Symptoms of iron deficiency anemia can include tiredness, weakness, lack of energy, and stomach upset[2]. In babies these signs can be hard to spot. If you’re concerned — or your baby eats very little iron-rich food — ask your pediatrician whether an iron check makes sense.

What to do

  1. When your baby shows readiness for solids, around 6 months, make iron foods among the first you offer[1][3].
  2. Aim to include an iron-rich food at most meals once your baby is eating regularly.
  3. Serve iron foods with a vitamin C food at the same meal[1].
  4. Prepare textures to match your baby’s skills: smooth or mashed at first, soft small pieces as they progress[3].
  5. Ask your pediatrician about iron screening and whether your baby needs a supplement, especially if exclusively breastfed. Don’t give iron supplements unless your pediatrician recommends them.

Frequently asked questions

When do babies need iron from food?

Starting around 6 months, when solids begin, guidance recommends choosing iron-containing foods. Breastfed babies may need a supplement earlier — ask your pediatrician[1].

Is infant rice cereal a good iron source?

Iron-fortified infant cereals are a common first food, but the CDC suggests offering a variety — oat, barley, and multigrain — rather than only rice cereal[3].

Can a vegetarian baby get enough iron?

It’s possible with planning: fortified cereal, beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, and greens, paired with vitamin C foods[1][2]. Talk with your pediatrician or a registered dietitian.

Does cow’s milk interfere with iron?

Cow’s milk isn’t recommended before 12 months, and afterward too much milk may make it harder to absorb iron[4]. Stick to recommended amounts.

Can a baby get too much iron?

From food, it’s unlikely. Iron supplements are a different story: accidental overdose of iron-containing products is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6, so keep supplements and adult vitamins out of reach[2].
