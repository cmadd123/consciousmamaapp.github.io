Which foods are choking hazards, and until what age?

Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

The riskiest foods are small, round, hard, or sticky: whole grapes, hot dogs, nuts and seeds, popcorn, raw carrot and apple chunks, chunks of cheese or meat, thick spoonfuls of nut butter, and hard or gummy candy. The AAP advises avoiding or modifying these until about age 4, depending on your child's development[1][2].

High-risk foods

Young children are still learning to chew, so foods that are small and round, hard, or sticky are the hardest for them to manage safely[1][2]. Most dangerous foods fit one of those shapes or textures — which makes them easier to spot.

Choking risk by age

Whole nuts deserve special mention: the AAP advises avoiding them until about age 4[3]. Peanut foods can still be introduced in infancy as thinned peanut butter.

How to make common foods safer

  • Grapes and cherry tomatoes: chop completely into tiny pieces rather than serving whole[1].
  • Hot dogs and sausages: chop completely into tiny pieces — never serve them in round coins[1].
  • Raw carrots and apples: cook until soft, or grate or shred them for young children[1][2].
  • Cheese: shred or cut into thin, small pieces instead of cubes or sticks[1].
  • Nut butter: spread thinly on toast or thin it into food; never offer it by the spoonful[1].
  • Meat: cook until tender and cut into small pieces or shred it[2].

What to do at every meal

  1. Supervise infants and young children whenever they eat[1].
  2. Have children sit down to eat — no walking, running, playing, or lying down with food in their mouth[1].
  3. Serve foods in a shape, size, and texture that matches your child’s skills[2].
  4. Watch older siblings, who may share unsafe foods or small objects with younger children[1].
  5. Learn infant and child choking first aid and CPR before you need it — many hospitals and the Red Cross offer classes.

Non-food hazards matter too: balloons (a risk up to age 8), coins, marbles, small toy parts, and button batteries, which can also cause internal burns[1].

Frequently asked questions

At what age can kids eat whole grapes?

The AAP lists whole grapes among foods to avoid or chop until about age 4, depending on your child’s development[1]. Until then, chop them completely into small pieces.

Is gagging the same as choking?

No. Gagging is common when babies learn to eat and is usually noisy — watery eyes, the tongue pushing forward, retching[5]. Choking is often silent: the child can’t cry, cough, or breathe. Choking needs immediate first aid.

Can toddlers eat popcorn?

Popcorn is on both the AAP and CDC lists of choking hazards for young children[1][2]. Most families wait until at least age 4.

How small should I cut food for a toddler?

The AAP recommends pieces no larger than ½ inch for infants and young children[1].

What are good low-risk foods for a new eater?

Soft foods that mash easily — ripe avocado or banana, well-cooked sweet potato, soft-cooked vegetables, shredded tender meat, and thinly spread nut butter — prepared in a size and shape that suits your child’s skills[2][4].
