How many times should you offer a new food to a toddler?

Published November 26, 2026 · Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Often 8 to 15 times. CDC says it may take 8 to 10 exposures before a young child will try a new food[1], the AAP says 10 or more tastes[2], and in one trial, 14 daily tastes of a disliked vegetable sharply increased how much preschoolers ate and liked it[3]. Keep portions tiny and pressure low.

Why repetition works

Young children are naturally wary of unfamiliar foods. Familiarity is what changes their minds: each low-pressure encounter makes the food a little less strange. That’s why a single refusal tells you very little. The NHS notes it can take 10 tries or more for a baby to get used to new foods, flavors, and textures[4].

The evidence is encouraging. In a randomized trial of about 440 three-year-olds, parents offered a tiny taste of a disliked vegetable every day for 14 days, with a sticker as a reward for tasting. Compared with a control group, children’s intake and liking of that vegetable rose significantly[3].

What counts as an exposure

  • Seeing it on the table or on someone else’s plate.
  • Having a small piece on their own plate, even if it isn’t touched.
  • Touching, smelling, or helping to prepare it.
  • Licking or tasting it — the strongest exposure.
  • Watching you eat and enjoy it. Eating the food first shows your child you like it[1].

Every one of these counts toward familiarity. A child who pokes broccoli tonight is closer to eating it than one who never sees it.

How to space the tries

There are two approaches, and both can work. At regular meals, CDC suggests waiting about a week before serving a refused food again[1], and the AAP suggests a week or two[2]. Separately, tiny daily tastes outside of meals, as in the trial above, can speed things up[3].

Why some children need more tries

Every child is different. Some children accept a food after a handful of tries; others need many more. Factors that can slow things down include strong reactions to texture, a recent bout of illness, teething, or simply a stubborn phase. Pressure tends to make things worse. Keep tries calm, keep portions small, and give it time. If your child accepts very few foods overall or is not growing well, talk to your pediatrician.

What to do

  1. Pick one or two "learning foods" at a time.
  2. Serve a very small amount next to familiar foods your child already likes.
  3. Eat the food yourself, without commentary.
  4. Don’t pressure your child or bribe them with treats for eating it[2].
  5. If it’s refused, remove it calmly and offer it again in a week or so[1][2].
  6. Vary the preparation — raw, roasted, in a sauce — once the basic version is familiar.
  7. Track tries on a list or in your meal plan so you don’t give up at try three.

Mealtime struggles rarely resolve overnight. It may take many tries before a child is ready to taste a new food, and a lot of tastes before they like it[5].

Frequently asked questions

When should I give up on a food?

There’s no firm cutoff. Many children need 8 to 10 or more exposures[1][2]. If a food is refused for months, take a break and try a new preparation later.

Is it bribing to use stickers for trying a food?

The trial that showed success used a small sticker for tasting[3]. The AAP advises against bribing with treats[2]. Non-food rewards for tasting are different from dessert for eating.

Does it count if my child just licks the food?

Yes. Seeing, touching, and licking all build familiarity. Tasting is the goal, but every interaction moves toward it.

How small should a new-food portion be?

Tiny — a pea-sized piece or a teaspoon. Small portions feel less overwhelming, and CDC even suggests freezing small bites to cut waste[1].

Should I offer the new food first, when my child is hungriest?

It can help to serve the new food alongside familiar foods at the start of a meal, when interest is highest. Keep the portion tiny and don’t make other foods conditional on tasting it.
